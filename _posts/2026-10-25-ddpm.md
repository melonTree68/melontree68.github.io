In this article, we introduce two different perspectives on Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM): one from stochastic analysis (continuous-time Markov chains, CTMC), and one from discrete-time Markov chains (used by the original DDPM paper [Ho et al. (2020)](#ref-ho2020denoising)). The material for the former is drawn from [Zhang (2025, Advanced Algorithms)](#ref-zhang2025aa7).

![Forward diffusion and reverse denoising processes in DDPM](/assets/img/posts/ddpm/ddpm.png){: w="700" }
*Forward diffusion and reverse denoising processes in DDPM (from [Ho et al. (2020)](#ref-ho2020denoising)).*

DDPM consists of two stages. The forward process systematically destroys a complex data sample by gradually adding Gaussian noise to it. The reverse process learns to denoise a noisy sample step by step to recover a sample from the data distribution (e.g., the uniform distribution on a large image dataset).

## The Stochastic Analysis Perspective

### The forward process

Consider an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process

$$
\d X_t = -X_t\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt2\,\mathrm{d}W_t,
\tag{1}\label{eq:ou}
$$

where $(W_t)_{t\ge0}$ is a standard Wiener process. Its solution is (by applying Itô's formula to $f(t,X_t)=e^tX_t$)

$$
X_t\overset{d}{=}e^{-t}X_0+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
\tag{2}\label{eq:ou_sol}
$$

As $t\to\infty$, $X_t$ converges in distribution to the standard Gaussian distribution. Hence, we can use $\eqref{eq:ou_sol}$ to simulate the OU process and systematically destroy a data sample $X_0$.

### The reverse process

To learn the denoising process, consider the time-reversed process of $\eqref{eq:ou}$

$$
Y_t=X_{T-t},\quad 0\le t\le T,
$$

where $T$ is chosen sufficiently large such that the law of $X_T$ is close to the standard Gaussian distribution. A tedious calculation (see [Zhang (2025, Stochastic Processes)](#ref-zhang2025sp14)) yields the SDE for the reverse process:

$$
\d Y_t = (Y_t + 2\nabla\log\pi_{T-t}(Y_t))\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt2\,\d\bar W_t,
\tag{3}\label{eq:reverse_ou}
$$

where $\pi_t$ is the density of $X_t$.

To simulate $\eqref{eq:reverse_ou}$ (e.g., using Euler–Maruyama discretization), it suffices to know the value of $\nabla\log\pi_t(x)$ (known as the score function) for every $t$ and $x$. However, this function is generally intractable to compute directly. So we approximate it using a neural network. The following formula makes it convenient to learn the score function.

<a id="tweedies-formula"></a>

{% capture thm_content %}
Let $Y = X + \sqrt t\cdot\epsilon$, where $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ is independent of $X$. Let $p_Y$ be the density of $Y$. Then

$$
\E[X \mid Y=y] = y + t\nabla\log p_Y(y).
$$

{% endcapture %}
{% include thms/theorem.html title="Tweedie's formula" content=thm_content %}

Recall from $\eqref{eq:ou_sol}$ that

$$
\underbrace{X_t}_Y = \underbrace{e^{-t}X_0}_X + \underbrace{\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}}_{\sqrt t}\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
$$

Since we can draw samples from the data distribution, we can construct $(x_0,x_t)$ pairs from the forward process, and train the network to predict $x_0$ from $x_t$ (and $t$). Use the ordinary MSE loss $\norm{x_0-\hat x_0}^2$. The solution to this easy optimization problem is

$$
\hat x_0^*(x_t,t) = \E[x_0 \mid x_t].
$$

Substituting it into [Tweedie's formula](#tweedies-formula) yields the score function.

## The Discrete-Time Markov Chain Perspective

The original implementation of DDPM differs from the approach described above in several ways. In particular, it uses discrete-time Markov chains instead of Itô diffusions to model the forward and reverse processes.

### Noise schedule

Instead of using a CTMC (OU process), the original DDPM selects a set of timesteps $(\tau_t)\_{0\le t\le T}$ and only considers the discrete-time Markov chain $x_t = X\_{\tau_t}$, $0\le t\le T$. Specifically, DDPM defines

$$
0<\alpha_t<1,\quad \beta_t = 1-\alpha_t,\quad \bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s,\quad 1\le t\le T,
$$

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
\tag{4}\label{eq:forward_one_step}
$$

Or, equivalently,

$$
x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon,\quad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I).
\tag{5}\label{eq:forward}
$$

Set $\tau_t = -\frac12\log\bar\alpha_t$ and we can see that $\eqref{eq:forward}$ is completely equivalent to $\eqref{eq:ou_sol}$. Hence, the noise schedule is just about selecting a discrete set of timesteps.

The original DDPM paper sets $T=1000$ and linearly increases $\beta_t$ from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$. The smallness of $\beta_t$ will be used in the asymptotic expansion below.

### Prediction target

DDPM usually directly predicts the noise in $\eqref{eq:forward}$ using a neural network $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$. The ordinary MSE loss is used:

$$
L_{\mathrm{simple}}=\E_{x_0,t,\epsilon}[\norm{\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}^2],\quad t\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,T\}.
$$

We show that this new prediction target is essentially just a reparameterization of score matching. Let $s_\theta(x_t,t)$ be the score estimator. The optimal noise predictor is $\epsilon^*(x_t,t)=\E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$. Take the conditional expectation of both sides of $\eqref{eq:forward}$ given $x_t$, and combine it with [Tweedie's formula](#tweedies-formula).

$$
s_\theta(x_t,t) = -\frac{\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},\qquad\hat x_0\mathrel{:=}\E[x_0\mid x_t]=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.
\tag{6}\label{eq:score_x0_pred}
$$

### Gaussian posterior

We will need the Gaussian posterior $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)$ in what follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0) &= \frac{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)q(x_t\mid x_{t-1},x_0)}{q(x_t\mid x_0)} \\
    \text{(Markov property)}\quad &= \frac{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}{q(x_t\mid x_0)} \\
    &= \phi\bra{\frac{x_{t-1}-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}}} \phi\bra{\frac{x_t-\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}}} \phi\bra{\frac{x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}}^{-1} \\
    \text{(by a tedious calculation)}\quad &= \mathcal{N}(\tilde\mu_t,\tilde\beta_tI),
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\phi$ is the standard Gaussian density, and

$$
\tilde\mu_t=\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t,\qquad\tilde\beta_t=\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t.
\tag{7}\label{eq:posterior}
$$

### Sampling

#### Definition

DDPM defines[^gaussian-posterior]

$$
p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\sigma_t^2I),
$$

where $\sigma_t^2$ usually equals $\beta_t$ or $\tilde\beta_t$, and

$$
\mu_\theta(x_t,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\bra{x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}.
\tag{8}\label{eq:post_mean}
$$

Here $\mu_\theta(x_t,t)$ is obtained by substituting $\hat x_0$ for $x_0$ in $\eqref{eq:posterior}$ (note that we no longer condition on $x_0$). During sampling, we start with $x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and sample

$$
x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t,t) + \sigma_tz,\quad t=T,\dots,1,
\tag{9}\label{eq:sample}
$$

where we resample $z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ at each step, and set $z=0$ at the final step $t=1$.

Note that although we substitute $\hat x_0$ for $x_0$ to obtain $\mu_\theta(x_t,t)$, which seems inaccurate, it still holds that $\mu^*(x_t,t)=\E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t]$:

$$
\E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t] = \E[\E[x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0]\mid x_t] = \E[\tilde\mu_t\mid x_t] = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\hat x_0+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t=\mu^*(x_t,t).
$$

#### Connection to the reverse SDE

We now show that the two perspectives are almost equivalent. Suppose $\sigma_t^2=\beta_t$. Combining $\eqref{eq:sample}$, $\eqref{eq:post_mean}$, and $\eqref{eq:score_x0_pred}$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{t-1} &= \frac{x_t+\beta_t s_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{1-\beta_t}} + \sqrt{\beta_t}z \\
    \text{(Taylor expand in $\beta_t$)}\quad &= x_t + \frac{\beta_t}{2}x_t + \beta_t s_\theta(x_t,t) + O(\beta_t^2) + \sqrt{\beta_t}z \\
    \text{($h\mathrel{:=}\beta_t/2$)}\quad &\approx x_t + (x_t + 2s_\theta(x_t,t))h + \sqrt{2h}z.
\end{aligned}
$$

This coincides with the Euler–Maruyama discretization of $\eqref{eq:reverse_ou}$. Moreover, the step size $h$ also matches that in the discretized forward process $\eqref{eq:forward_one_step}$:

$$
h_{\mathrm{forward}} = \tau_t - \tau_{t-1} = -\frac12\log(1-\beta_t)\approx\frac{\beta_t}{2}.
$$

### Loss function

#### Reparameterization changes the loss weights across timesteps

[The reverse process](#the-reverse-process) uses equally weighted original-image MSE, while now we use equally weighted noise MSE. $\eqref{eq:score_x0_pred}$ shows the difference between the two:

$$
\norm{\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta}^2 = \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\norm{x_0-\hat x_0}^2.
$$

Therefore, noise MSE is the signal-to-noise ratio-weighted original-image MSE. The reparameterization changes the loss weights across timesteps and shifts the focus of training.

#### An ELBO-based loss function

We can use the evidence lower bound (ELBO) to derive an alternative loss function. To do this, define the model's joint distribution $p_\theta(x_{0:T})$ and the forward distribution $q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)$. They factorize as

$$
p_\theta(x_{0:T}) = \phi(x_T)\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t),\qquad q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0) = q(x_T\mid x_0)\prod_{t=2}^{T}q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0).
\tag{10}\label{eq:factorize}
$$

Here we use the Markov property. Now ELBO gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\log p_\theta(x_0) &\ge \E_{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\sbra{\log\frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}} \\
    &= \E_{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\sbra{\log\frac{\phi(x_T)}{q(x_T\mid x_0)}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)}+\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)} \\
    &= -L_{\mathrm{VLB}},
\end{aligned}
$$

where VLB stands for variational lower bound and

$$
L_{\mathrm{VLB}} = \E\sbra{\underbrace{\KL{q(x_T\mid x_0)}{\phi(x_T)}}_{L_T} + \sum_{t=2}^{T}\underbrace{\KL{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)}{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}}_{L_{t-1}} - \underbrace{\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)}_{\text{reconstruction loss}}}.
$$

Let's look at $L_T$ first. Because

$$
q(x_T\mid x_0)=\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}x_0,(1-\bar\alpha_T)I),\qquad p(x_T)=\mathcal{N}(0,I),
$$

$L_T$ does not depend on $\theta$ and thus can be ignored during training.

Now consider $L_{t-1}$. We already know that

$$
q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)=\mathcal{N}(\tilde\mu_t,\tilde\beta_tI),\qquad p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta,\sigma_t^2I).
$$

Hence

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac{\norm{\tilde\mu_t-\mu_\theta}^2}{2\sigma_t^2}+\underbrace{\frac d2\bra{\frac{\tilde\beta_t}{\sigma_t^2}-1+\log\frac{\sigma_t^2}{\tilde\beta_t}}}_{\text{not depending on $\theta$}}.
$$

$$
L_{t-1}=\E_{x_0,\epsilon}\sbra{\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\norm{\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}^2}+C_t.
$$

Here $d$ is the data dimension and $C_t$ is a constant that does not depend on $\theta$.

Now consider the reconstruction loss. Direct calculation yields

$$
L_0=\E_{x_0,\epsilon}\sbra{\frac{\beta_1}{2\sigma_1^2\alpha_1}\norm{\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}^2}+C.
$$

Note that in the original DDPM implementation, $L_{\mathrm{simple}}$ is adopted instead of this fancier ELBO-based loss $L_{\mathrm{VLB}}$, because the former just works better.
